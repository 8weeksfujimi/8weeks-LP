  • FJM-JOMON-001
    Grade S
    Idojiri Sites (Idojiri and Sori Sites) and the Idojiri Culture

    A core Middle Jomon settlement site on a plateau at about 880 m on the southern foot of the Yatsugatake range. Idojiri was designated a National Historic Site in 1966, and in 2025 the adjoining Sori site was added and the designation renamed 'Idojiri Sites'. The Idojiri Archaeological Museum holds the finds, including a set of 199 items from the nearby Tonai site (Important Cultural Property, 2002) and a figurine from the Sakaue site (Important Cultural Property, 2015).

    FJM-JOMON-001 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-002
    Grade A
    The Jomon Agriculture Theory (Fujimori Eiichi and the Idojiri Site)

    Fujimori Eiichi (1911-1973), an independent archaeologist born in Kamisuwa (now Suwa), proposed in 1950, after investigating the Idojiri site, that people of the Middle Jomon Central Highlands lived partly by farming. His reading of perforated-collar pots as seed containers was nearly ignored by academics, and he died in 1973 with its worth unsettled. Carbonized seeds thought to be egoma found in 1974, and later reports of managed cultivation of nuts and millets, have prompted a reassessment, but it remains a hypothesis under study rather than an established view.

    FJM-JOMON-002 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-003
    Grade A
    Deep Pot with Swirling Water-Smoke Pattern

    One of the vessels excavated from Pit Dwelling No. 4 at the Sori site; seven deep pots found together were designated Nagano Prefectural Treasures in 1975, not a national designation. Its swirling, rising handles are likened to flames or water mist. It was shown at an exhibition of Japanese art in Paris in 1963 and used in 1972 as the design of the printed stamp on postcards.

  • FJM-JOMON-004
    Grade S
    Finds from the Tonai Site (Cylindrical Pot with Deity Figure)

    A page on the Tonai site (Sakai, Fujimi), one of the Idojiri Sites; the Nagamine site used for comparison is in Chino, outside the town. The Tonai finds are not a prefectural treasure but a set designated an Important Cultural Property in 2002, comprising 48 pottery and clay items and 151 stone tools, 199 in all, and assessed as a peak of modelling in the Middle Jomon Central Highlands. Its symbol is a cylindrical pot 55.7 cm high on which a human-shaped deity figure clings, with a large round hole, taken to be an eye, in its head.

    FJM-JOMON-004 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-005
    Grade A
    The Idojiri Chronology and Muto Yuroku

    The Idojiri chronology is a Middle Jomon pottery sequence for the Central Highlands running Kyubeione, Mujinazawa, Shindo, Tonai, Idojiri and Sori styles, all named for sites of the Idojiri Sites within Fujimi; it is said to have been systematised by Fujimori Eiichi and others in the early 1960s and established in the 1965 report 'Idojiri' (some place the first presentation around 1963). Muto Yuroku, the first director of the Idojiri Archaeological Museum, was born in Ikebukuro, Tokyo, in 1930 and is said to have entered archaeology by collecting arrowheads while recovering from tuberculosis. The story that he was 'expelled' by Fujimori three times is hearsay lacking primary-source support.

    FJM-JOMON-005 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-006
    Grade A
    Snake Designs at Idojiri (Snake-Pattern Pots and the Half-Human, Half-Frog Perforated-Collar Pot)

    The centre of this entry is the Idojiri Sites in Sakai, Fujimi, but the Togariishi site used for comparison is in Toyohira, Chino, outside the town. Pots bearing snake designs, such as snake-patterned deep pots, have been excavated from the Idojiri Sites, and the Idojiri Archaeological Museum, which holds about 2,000 items, builds its displays around deciphering the frogs, half-human frogs, snakes and moons in the patterns. The half-human, half-frog perforated-collar pot from the Tonai site was excavated by the Idojiri Preservation Society in 1962 and is an Important Cultural Property; Fujimori Eiichi and Muto Yuroku proposed in 1963 that it was a seed jar, a view Muto revised in 1970 to a sake-brewing vessel.

    FJM-JOMON-006 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-007
    Grade A
    Bread-like Carbonized Material from the Sori Site (Birthplace of 'Jomon Cookies')

    In the scholarly history of baked food known as 'Jomon cookies', the analysis dispute over the Oshidashi site in Takahata, Yamagata (outside Fujimi), is often cited, but the term itself derives from cookie-like or bread-like carbonized material from nuts first excavated at the Sori site in Fujimi, from the Middle Jomon. It was found in the first survey of 1960 in Pit Dwelling No. 5 (mid-Middle Jomon, Sori I style), and Muto Yuroku reconstructed the scattered fragments over many years. SEM observation by Matsutani Akiko, held at the University Museum of the University of Tokyo, shows grains over 1 mm long and the 'straw-sandal-shaped cells' characteristic of perilla (egoma) seeds.

    FJM-JOMON-007 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-008
    Grade B
    Jomon Iconography and the Perforated-Collar Pot with Half-Human, Half-Frog Pattern (Idojiri Archaeological Museum)

    It should be said first that the work of Tanaka Motoi, one of the interpreters discussed, extends beyond Fujimi to the whole Suwa region including Chino. The Idojiri Archaeological Museum has taken its own approach, Jomon iconography, treating pottery motifs not as decoration but as 'images' and reading the spiritual world of Middle Jomon people through myth and folklore. A set including the perforated-collar pot with half-human, half-frog pattern from the Tonai site (Sakai, Fujimi) is designated a national Important Cultural Property, but the museum's mythic reading is its own and not an established view in the field.

    FJM-JOMON-008 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-009
    Grade A
    Pot with Painted Human Figure from the Todonomiya Site (Idojiri Sites)

    The Idojiri Sites comprise seven sites, including Idojiri, Sori, Tonai, Kyubeione, Idaira and Todonomiya, a group of settlements centred on the mid-Middle Jomon on ridges and plateaus at 800-1,000 m on the southern foot of Yatsugatake. At the Todonomiya site a large deep pot, 50 cm in diameter and 63 cm high, was found in 1968 during farm-road widening, with a human figure painted in black pigment near its base; it is a Fujimi town tangible cultural property held at the Idojiri Archaeological Museum (Sakai, Fujimi). Its reading is not settled: Agency for Cultural Affairs material itself gives both a childbirth theory and a theory linking it to death and burial.

    FJM-JOMON-009 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-010
    Grade A
    The Obsidian Road (Hoshikuso Pass, Hoshigato and Idojiri)

    The source side of the obsidian road, namely Hoshikuso Pass (Nagawa), Hoshigato (Shimosuwa) and Wada Pass (on the Nagawa/Shimosuwa border), lies entirely outside Fujimi; within the town is the receiving side around Idojiri. At the Hoshikuso Pass obsidian source site (Nagawa, about 1,500 m), investigation confirmed 195 mining pits about 10 m across and a protected area of 6.6 ha, and it was designated a National Historic Site in 2001, while at the Hoshigato site in Shimosuwa (1,500 m) 193 mining pits are spread over about 35,000 square metres. From Building No. 3 at the Idojiri site, 43 chipped stone axes and 10 hollow stones have been found, though the material is not stated to be obsidian.

    FJM-JOMON-010 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-011
    Grade A
    Kyubeione Site and the Settlement Layout of the Idojiri Sites (Ridges, Valleys and Springs)

    The Idojiri Sites are said to consist of the Idojiri, Sori, Tonai, Kyubeione and Idaira sites and others, but their contents and boundaries vary by source. Dwelling remains, centred on the Middle Jomon, lie scattered and overlapping across each ridge and terrace group, and the Kyubeione site contains remains from the Early to Middle Jomon; the 'Kyubeione style' of pottery named for it is part of the Idojiri chronology and is also used as a dating scale in reports on the Togariishi and Yosukeone sites in neighbouring Chino. Since 1949 several excavations have confirmed well over a hundred dwelling remains across the group.

    FJM-JOMON-011 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-012
    Grade A
    The Debate over the Use of the Perforated-Collar Pot (Seed-Storage, Sake-Brewing and Drum Theories)

    The perforated-collar pot is a vessel type with a row of small holes at the rim and a collar-like ridge around the middle of the body, distributed from the Central Highlands to the Kanto, mainly in the Middle Jomon. In 1963 Fujimori Eiichi and Muto Yuroku, from the Jomon agriculture viewpoint, read it as a seed container, and in 1964 Yamanouchi Sugao proposed a percussion-instrument theory, comparing the snake and human motifs and the hole arrangement to clay drums around the world; in 1970 Muto himself changed his view to a sake-brewing vessel. In 2023 a complete bamboo-tube-shaped example was excavated from a dwelling in Trench 9 of the Sori site.

    FJM-JOMON-012 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-013
    Grade S
    Birth and Rebirth Imagery Read in Face-Shaped Handles and the Sakaue Site Figurine

    'Face-handled pottery' is the general term for Middle Jomon deep pots with a human-face projection attached to the rim; the first example is said to have been found in 1904 at Yukawa, Kitayama Village, Suwa District (now Chino, outside Fujimi). Centred on the Katsusaka (Idojiri) culture, they became large and standardised from the end of the Tonai period to the Idojiri period but survive only on incense-burner-type pots in the Sori period; readings as goddess worship, a Hainuwele-type myth or a rite of rebirth are only views within the field. The standing figurine from the Sakaue site was designated an Important Cultural Property in 2015, but the designation text does not mention birth or rebirth.

    FJM-JOMON-013 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-014
    Grade A
    Decline of Settlements at the End of the Middle Jomon (Yatsugatake Foothills, Central Highlands) and the Idojiri Sites

    The Middle Jomon settlements packed onto the Yatsugatake foothills fell rapidly in number after the Sori period and never regained their former density in the Late Jomon. A similar decline is known to have occurred at about the same time across wide areas of the Kanto and Chubu at the end of the Middle Jomon, as the archaeologist Taniguchi Yasuhiro traced through studies of ring-shaped settlements, and why it happened is still not settled: climatic cooling shrinking food resources, a limit reached by Jomon subsistence, and people moving elsewhere are all argued without agreement. Specialist journals have also recently questioned dating links between the decline and the 4.2 ka event.

    FJM-JOMON-014 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-015
    Grade A
    Idojiri Archaeological Museum and Its Local Preservation History (Sakai Historical Society, Idojiri Preservation Society, Successive Directors, Idojiri Supporters)

    The excavation of Idojiri began not with an office or a university but with the local 'Sakai Historical Society' and students of Suwa Seiryo High School. In March 1958, under the guidance of the independent archaeologist Fujimori Eiichi and Miyasaka Hideichi of the Togariishi site, a young man who joined the trial excavation was Muto Yuroku, later the first director, and in September of that year pottery was already being restored and displayed, said to be the origin of the museum. Management passed to the town board of education in 1965 and the present building opened in 1974, and local volunteers known as the Idojiri Supporters now help with tasks such as tending the lotus pond.

    FJM-JOMON-015 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-JOMON-016
    Grade A
    Springs and the Siting of Jomon Settlements (Idojiri Spring, the Spring Line on the Southern Yatsugatake)

    On a long, narrow plateau at 800 to 1,000 m on the southern foot of Yatsugatake (900-1,000 m in academic sources), Middle Jomon settlements of about 5,000 years ago, including Idojiri, Sori, Tonai, Kyubeione, Idaira and Todonomiya, crowd together over several kilometres. The name 'Idojiri' is thought to derive from a spring that still wells just east of the site, and Agency for Cultural Affairs materials also state that the settlement stood on the ridge where this spring rises; rainwater soaked into the volcano seeps out along the boundaries of lava layers of differing permeability. The layout of settlements divided by small valleys looks like traces of people choosing to live along the line where water wells up.

    FJM-JOMON-016 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-001
    Grade A
    Takeda Harunobu (Shingen)'s Invasion of Shinano and the Fall of the Suwa Clan

    The main scenes, Kuwabara Castle in Suwa and Toko-ji in Kofu, lie outside the town. In the sixth month of 1542 Takeda Harunobu, later known as Shingen, advanced into Suwa District; Suwa Yorishige surrendered Kuwabara Castle on the fourth day of the seventh month on condition that his life be spared, but on the 21st Harunobu broke the promise and had Yorishige and his brother Yoritaka take their own lives at Toko-ji, ending the Suwa main line (sources differ on where he was held).

    FJM-HIST-001 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-002
    Grade A
    Battle of Sezawa (1542)

    On the ninth day of the third month of 1542, Takeda Harunobu is said to have met an allied force of the 'four great commanders' of Shinano (Ogasawara, Suwa, Murakami, Kiso) at Sezawa on the Kai-Shinano border, from present-day Ochiai Sezawa to Yokobuki in Fujimi. A local signboard records that 1,621 Shinano men were killed in a battle lasting from the hour of the Dragon to the hour of the Sheep, and the battlefield was given local historic-site status in 1965. No reliable primary source records the battle, and some suggest it was invented later by the Takeda to justify their conquest of Suwa.

    FJM-HIST-002 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-003
    Grade A
    Shingen's Bomichi Road (Upper, Middle, Lower)

    The stretch passing through Fujimi is very short; the main traces and the roadside Kannon stones lie in neighbouring Hokuto City. The Edo-period gazetteer Kai Kokushi (1814) says only that there are three 'bo' roads and does not credit Shingen with building them. Only the upper road is certain to survive; traces of the middle and lower roads are scarce, and the route and the origin of the road remain debated.

    FJM-HIST-003 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-004
    Grade A
    Koroku River (Border River of Kai and Shinano)

    A small river of the Fuji River system, about 9 km long, rising at about 1,420 m on the southern slope of Mount Amikasa and joining the Kamanashi River beneath the new Kokkai Bridge on the Koshu road. It forms the present prefectural border between Fujimi, Nagano, and Hokuto, Yamanashi, and Fujimi's official materials say 'bounded by the Kamanashi and Koroku Rivers'; however, which river formed the border in the Sengoku period, the Sakai, Tatsuba or Koroku, is disputed.

    FJM-HIST-004 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-005
    Grade A
    Sendatsu Castle (Kohigashi Castle, Shingoro Yashiki) and Castle Traditions of Okkoto

    On the western foot of Yatsugatake in Shinano-Sakai, the ruins of Sendatsu Castle remain as a town-designated historic site. Also called Kohigashi Castle or Shingoro Yashiki, it is said to be the 'Shingoro Yashiki of Kohigashi' that a Suwa shrine record (『諏訪神使御頭之日記』) says Takeda Nobutora took when attacking Suwa in 1528, though who Shingoro was is still unknown. The lord is traditionally said to be a Takeda retainer, Tada Awaji-no-kami, but no primary source names him, and the Nihon Jokaku Taikei suggests it was a relay point on the western-foot road rather than a defensive castle.

    FJM-HIST-005 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-HIST-007
    Grade A
    Misayama Godo Village, Godo Shinmachi and the Lineage of 'Shinden' Villages

    The earliest documentary mention of Misayama Godo (now in Fujimi) goes back to an entry in the text 祝祠段 (reading unconfirmed) with a colophon dated 1237. In the early modern period the settlement moved from the old town to Shinmachi along the Koshu road and became an intermediate stop between Kanazawa and Tsutaki; a joint-signed letter of 1611 from Suwa senior retainers is said to have encouraged house-building in Shinmachi, but whether it was issued under Yorimitsu or Yorimizu is not settled even in the original. A report of 1733 records 126 houses, and the word 'shinden' never appears in that source.

    FJM-HIST-007 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-001
    Grade S
    Okkoto Suwa Shrine (Suwa-zukuri, Important Cultural Property)

    The shrine buildings are the worship hall and offering hall of the Suwa Taisha Kamisha Honmiya, relocated here and said to date from 1617. When the Kamisha began rebuilding in 1833, the village of Okkoto asked to take over the old buildings, and the move was completed in 1849. With no main hall and the rock behind the shrine serving as the seat of the deity, it keeps the 'Suwa-zukuri' form strongly and is one of few such buildings in Japan.

    FJM-FAITH-001 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-002
    Grade B
    The Old Layers of Suwa Belief (Mishaguji and Mount Moriya)

    Mount Moriya and Suwa Taisha itself lie outside Fujimi, and no direct physical evidence has been found within the town. Suwa Taisha is traditionally said to preserve an old form of worship without a main hall, though of its four shrines the Maemiya has one, and the earliest textual reference to Mount Moriya as the sacred body of the Honmiya dates only to 1553, so that idea may be a fairly recent layer. The native deity Mishaguji, said to be older than Takeminakata, likewise has several competing explanations of its name and no settled view.

    FJM-FAITH-002 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-003
    Grade A
    Godo Hachiman Shrine (with Suwa Myojin as Co-enshrined Deity) and the Misayama Shrine of Godo

    Godo Hachiman Shrine and the Misayama Shrine of Godo stand within Fujimi (Misayama Godo), though the old Misayama of the Suwa Taisha Kamisha auxiliary shrine mentioned later is said to lie in Hara Village, so its grove may span both. The main hall of the Hachiman shrine, in the village along the old Koshu road, is a single-bay nagare-zukuri building built in 1762 by the carpenter Kato Kichizaemon and designated a town tangible cultural property; its deity is said to be Oyamatsumi. A 1969 history of the shrine calls it a 'special building' that enshrines Suwa Myojin in the co-enshrined seat on the left.

    FJM-FAITH-003 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-004
    Grade B
    Village-by-Village Onbashira (Komiya Festival) and Okkoto Suwa Shrine

    The offering hall (heiden) of Okkoto Suwa Shrine, built in 1617, was designated a National Treasure on 23 May 1930 (now an Important Cultural Property); it is a Suwa-zukuri building with no main hall, whose offering-and-worship hall serves that function. It is said to have been moved to Okkoto in the Kaei era, but sources disagree on whether the year was 1849, 1853 or unknown. In the Suwa region even small shrines (komiya) raise onbashira pillars in a 'Komiya festival' that passes on the technique.

    FJM-FAITH-004 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-005
    Grade A
    Roadside Stone Deities (Dosojin of Tatsuzawa and the Hundred Koshin of Wakamiya)

    The Fujimi Board of Education's list of designated cultural properties includes the Dosojin of the 中下組 group in Tatsuzawa and the 'Hundred Koshin' of Wakamiya, both as structures. The Hundred Koshin is said to have been set up by the then 63 households of Wakamiya Shinden, at the initiative of the village official Hosokawa Toshiro, after an epidemic and great flood in 1860, and moved slightly south and repaired in 1986 for a road improvement; this account is local tradition from an individual's research and has not been corroborated in town histories.

    FJM-FAITH-005 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-006
    Grade A
    Misayama Shrine Festival and Kajikimen (Suwa Hunting Rite)

    Suwa Taisha Kamisha, the centre of the Kajikimen and the Ontosai, lies outside Fujimi (in Suwa and Chino), but Misayama Shrine, which preserves its hunting faith, is a town-designated historic site in Misayama (Godo district), Fujimi. In the August Misayama festival the deities Kunitokotachi no Mikoto and Suwa Okami are welcomed from the Kamisha and a hut of susuki grass is built, where priests and warriors keep a vigil, offering game and praying for a good harvest. Known as 'Harayama-sama', it also doubles as a prayer for the health of two-year-olds by Japanese reckoning, and sources differ on whether the long-lived fish released into the river afterwards is an eel or a loach.

    FJM-FAITH-006 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-007
    Grade A
    Weeping Cherry at Takamori Kannon Hall

    A weeping cherry on Mount Atago in Sakai Takamori, designated a Fujimi town natural monument (plant) on 1 July 1967. The base of its trunk has grown around the remains of a stone column, and its age is estimated at about 250 years; however, records vary on the trunk girth at eye level (3.7 or 3.8 m) and on the height (about 15 or about 10 m).

    FJM-FAITH-007 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-008
    Grade A
    Okkoto-segi Weir and the Water-Use Traditions of 'Segi' (Sakamoto Yosen, Hienosoko)

    Introductory note: Takinoyu-segi and Ogawara-segi are background technical information from Chino and lie outside Fujimi. Okkoto-segi was conceived by Sakamoto Yosen, who submitted six opinions to the Suwa domain over eight years, and was completed in 1792 as a channel of over 6 km taking water from the Tatsuba River; where it crosses Koroku-segi the two are built as a grade-separated crossing so that the water does not mix, which is said to be unusual locally. Each year in mid-May representatives of Tatsuzawa and Okkoto districts attend, check the stones and adjust the flow so that the water is shared equally.

    FJM-FAITH-008 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-009
    Grade A
    The Moriya Clan, the Jinchokan and the Dual System of Suwa Kamisha (Ohori / Jinchokan)

    The Ohori's residence site (Nakasu, Suwa) and the Jinchokan Moriya Historical Museum (Miyagawa, Chino), central to this record, both lie outside Fujimi, and the only geographic link to the town is largely the Misayama festival (27 August each year, Misayama, Fujimi). At Suwa Taisha Kamisha the Ohori (hereditary from the Suwa family), regarded as the living deity and vessel of Takeminakata, is described as the 'chief holding political power', and the Jinchokan, hereditary in the Moriya clan, as the 'priest with ritual authority', a dual system (a 'dual kingship').

    FJM-FAITH-009 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-010
    Grade A
    Ontosai (Omitachi-za Shinji) and the Ear-Split Deer of Koya

    The main venue, the Maemiya and Jikkenro of Suwa Taisha Kamisha, and the Jinchokan Moriya Historical Museum with its reconstructed display, are all in Miyagawa, Chino, outside Fujimi. The Ontosai (festival of the Rooster) is the Kamisha's grand festival, formally called 'Omitachi-za Shinji'; deer heads were once offered, but the number varies by source, with Kaibara Ekiken's 'Kisoji no Ki' (1713) giving seventy-five among the oldest records and Sugae Masumi's 'Suwa no Umi' (1784) giving seventy-two. Sugae wrote that one of the heads he saw was of a deer with a split ear, the 'ear-split deer of Koya' (神野, also written 高野), one of the Seven Wonders of Suwa Taisha.

    FJM-FAITH-010 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-011
    Grade A
    Frog-Hunting Rite (Suwa Taisha Kamisha Honmiya)

    The rite takes place not in Fujimi but at the Suwa Taisha Kamisha Honmiya in neighbouring Suwa (outside the town). On New Year's Day the ice of the frozen Mitarashi River is broken, and a frog hiding beneath is caught, shot with a willow bow and arrow, and offered; a frog is said always to be found even in midwinter, and the rite has been counted among the Seven Wonders of Suwa Taisha.

    FJM-FAITH-011 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-012
    Grade B
    The Struggle Between Moriya and Takeminakata (the Wisteria Kagi and the Iron Kagi)

    The shrines central to this tradition lie outside Fujimi: Moriya Shrine in Kawagishi-higashi, Okaya, Fujishima Shrine in Kawagishi-kami, Okaya, and the Moriya residence (Jinchokan Moriya Historical Museum) in Miyagawa Takabe, Chino; the only firm point of contact in the town is Misayama Shrine (Fujimi Tomihara, about 985 m, enshrining Takeminakata no Mikoto and Kunitokotachi no Mikoto). The medieval 'Suwa Nobushige Gejo' (1249) records that the deity (Takeminakata) held a wisteria kagi and the minister an iron kagi, contested the land here, and the deity won and put down Minister Moriya (said to be the god Moriya).

    FJM-FAITH-012 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-FAITH-013
    Grade A
    Godo Hachiman Shrine and the Shrine 柏尾社 (Border Clan Shrines of Fujimi, and Suwa / Hachiman / Takeda Motifs)

    All are clan shrines within Fujimi (Misayama Godo Hachiman Shrine and Fujimi Suwa Shrine in the district of Fujimi, and 柏尾社, reading unconfirmed, and Shinmei Shrine in the district of Sakai). A 1969 history of Godo Hachiman Shrine describes it as a 'special building' enshrining Suwa Myojin on the left of the co-enshrined seat, with the Suwa Kamisha crest 'Suwa kaji' on the doors and a pair of deer on the Suwa side (left) and doves on the Hachiman side (right) carved on the frog-leg struts of the main body. The master carpenter was Kato Kichizaemon, and it was built in 1762 and is a Fujimi town tangible cultural property.

  • FJM-LEGEND-001
    Grade B
    The Kuzui Pure Pond and the 'Underground Passage Legend'

    A tradition concerning the pond of Kuzui Shrine, an auxiliary shrine of the Suwa Taisha Kamisha in Chino, outside Fujimi. On New Year's Eve the offerings, sakaki, willow branches and oak leaves used at the Kamisha during the year are sunk into the pond at the Hour of the Tiger, signalled by the lamp at the Maemiya, in a rite (御手幣送り) recorded in the 14th-century 'Suwa Daimyojin Ekotoba' (1356). The sunken items are said to be connected underground and to surface on New Year's morning at the 'Sanagi pond' in Totomi (Shizuoka), one of the Seven Wonders of Suwa; Sakuragaike in Omaezaki is a leading candidate, but the identification is not settled.

    FJM-LEGEND-001 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-LEGEND-002
    Grade B
    Site of the Old Village of Hienosoko

    A small village at about 1,200 m on the Yatsugatake foothills, in Tatsuzawa along the Bomichi road, recorded in the land surveys of 1590 (36 koku 3 sho) and 1613. Because of cold weather that spoiled the fields and damage from wild boar and deer, it moved to Tatsuzawa Shinden in the 1640s, returned in the 1650s and fell into hardship again; a shogunate document of 1763 records the final move to Okkoto and Tatsuzawa. The town designated it a town historic site in 1965, and later retellings of an eerie incident there are not supported by primary sources.

    FJM-LEGEND-002 (full text in Japanese)
  • FJM-LEGEND-003
    Grade C
    The Rock Altar of Osaki Shrine (大先神社) and the Mysterious Stone Monument

    A small shrine called Osaki Shrine (大先神社, reading unconfirmed) remains in the Hienosoko forest in Tatsuzawa, Fujimi.     Bee-Chasing (Sugare-oi)

    In the mountains of Fujimi there are still people who chase sugare, the small ground-nesting wasp known as kurosuzumebachi.     Drawing Thread from Karamushi (Ramie): The Oldest Layer of the Lineage of Thread

    Regional records say that drawing thread from karamushi (ramie) grown on the grounds of the Idojiri Archaeological Museum continues today. The cut stalks are soaked in water to loosen the skin, the stripped bark is scraped on a board with a spatula, and the remaining fibre is split fine and twisted into thread; only the tools have changed to metal, and the outline of the process is said to be little different from Jomon times. Ramie is among the oldest plant fibres used in the Japanese archipelago, with Final Jomon woven cloth (angin) found at about twelve sites, though no material survives from the Yayoi to the Kamakura period and how the technique continued is unknown.

    The centre of this page is an event inside Fujimi, but the related 'Inochi no Matsuri 2000' (Omachi, Nagano) and the current base of the executive committee chair Oe Masanori (Hokuto, Yamanashi) are treated as outside the town. In August 1988 the outdoor gathering 'Inochi no Matsuri', prompted by the Chernobyl accident, was held under the theme 'NO NUKES ONE LOVE', with Oe Masanori as chair of the executive committee. Yamada Kaiya's 'I Am a Hippie' places the venue at the Fujimi Panorama Ski Area at the foot of the Southern Alps (a single first-hand account), and sources differ on the duration and attendance.

    This page includes elements outside Fujimi and unconfirmed links to the town.     Fujimi Mori-no-Office (Fujimi Telework Town Initiative)

    In December 2015 a building that had been a Musashino University retreat was leased and renovated and opened as the shared office 'Mori no Office', with private rooms, coworking space, a dining room and a kitchen; it is run by Route Design LLC. With housing subsidies already in place against population decline, the town is said to have shifted its emphasis to creating places to work.

    Fujimi Panorama Resort (MTB and the All Japan Championships)

    A resort on the eastern slope of Mount Nyugasa in Fujimi, whose eight-person gondola, up to 3,000 m long with a vertical drop of 730 m, carries skiers in winter and in summer becomes a lift for bikes. The All Japan Mountain Bike Championships have been held here ten times, the most of any single venue (next is Tazawako, Akita, with five), and the 33rd championships in 2020 were moved here at short notice from Tazawako. It has a permanent downhill course certified by the UCI and the JCF.

    Grade A
    Fujimi Town Library (Highest Loans in Japan)

    A small town library on the southern foot of Yatsugatake that long stood near the top nationwide in loans per resident. According to a National Diet Library news article, in fiscal 2008 it lent 20.5 items per resident, first among town and village libraries for the 12th straight year; in fiscal 2010 it lent 18.0 and was first for the 14th straight year in the 15,000-20,000 population class, and fifth nationwide regardless of size. A local essay speaks of '15 straight years', which does not simply match these official figures and has not been verified.

    Grade A
    Suzuran-no-Sato Station (Petition Station, Town Flower Lily of the Valley)

    Mount Nyugasa and its wetland, which gave the station its name, are said to span Fujimi, Chino and Ina, so this entry includes areas outside the town. Suzuran-no-Sato Station opened on 31 October 1985 as an unstaffed, passenger-only petition station of Japanese National Railways, its cost of about 200 million yen borne entirely by Seiko Epson (Fujimi and Suwa-minami plants); the name was decided at Epson's request, without precedent for the railway and said to have caused controversy, and derives from 'Suzuran Kogen', another name for the Nyugasa highland. Lily of the valley was designated the town flower in 1984.

    SEIMITSU FUJIMI: The Town's Will to Begin Recording Its Own Industry

    In autumn 2022 the website 'SEIMITSU FUJIMI' began, under the headline 'Fujimi, Town of Precision', prompted by a broadcast from the Suwa Area Industrial Messe. Since then it has visited the workplaces of precision machining, moulds, die-casting and jewellery in the town one company at a time, accumulating nearly thirty interview articles with owners and successors (still updating in autumn 2025); its footer links to the town's chamber of commerce, but the operator is not named on the site, and its aim is to attract firms and promote sales channels.

  • FJM-MOD-008
    Grade A
    Kushi (Village Histories): The Culture of Hamlets Writing Their Own History

    Fujimi has some forty hamlets (ku), and several of them have compiled their own village histories apart from the town's official history. In Kuzukubo, Sakai, three volunteers began sorting materials, a compilation committee took it up in earnest in 2009, and the 'Kuzukubo Kushi' was published in 2013, compiled from the starting point of having lost most of its old documents in the 1938 fire at Sakai Elementary School. Ikebukuro published its village history in 1970, and Eboshi began compiling its own in 2011, still at work in 2022.
